# The Pledges at al-‘Aqabah

chapter: hch_aqaba
stage: setting
prefix: sp_aqaba_chapter_

**Question:** How did a handful of pilgrims from Yathrib open a home for the Prophet ﷺ and his companions?

## Overview

Every pilgrimage season the Prophet ﷺ went out to the Arab tribes and presented himself to them, and one season, at a place called al-‘Aqabah, a few men from Yathrib sat down to listen.[[01:p1–p9 | Ibn Ishaq]][[03:p1–p3, 04:p1–p4 | Ibn Ishaq, "as has been related to me": the six and their names]] Their own people at home were torn apart by feuds.[[02:p1 | ‘Asim's elders, continued]] What those men carried back, and what was promised at al-‘Aqabah in the seasons that followed, would decide whether the Prophet ﷺ had anywhere to go, and what his hosts would risk for him.[[21:p1–p4 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting the Prophet ﷺ, al-Bara' and Abu al-Haytham]][[24:p1–p4 | ‘Asim ibn ‘Umar ibn Qatadah, quoting al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah and the Prophet ﷺ]]

## Six men at al-‘Aqabah

Every pilgrimage season, the Prophet ﷺ presented himself to the Arab tribes who came for it. One season, at al-‘Aqabah, he met a few men of Khazraj. "Will you not sit down, so that I can speak with you?" he asked. They sat, and he called them to Allah and recited the Quran to them.[[01:p1–p9 | Ibn Ishaq]]

The word "prophet" was not new to them. At home they lived beside Jews, people of scripture, and in every quarrel the Jews would say: "A prophet is about to be sent; his time has drawn near. We will follow him, and with him we will kill you as ‘Ad and Iram were killed," peoples of old.[[01:p1–p9 | Ibn Ishaq]][[02:p1 | ‘Asim's elders, continued]] Now the men turned to one another. "By Allah, this is the prophet the Jews threatened you with. Do not let them reach him before you."[[02:p1 | ‘Asim's elders, continued]]

They accepted Islam. "No people are as torn by enmity as ours," they told him. "Perhaps Allah will unite them through you. If He does, no man will be more honoured than you."[[02:p1 | ‘Asim's elders, continued]] There were six of them, among them As‘ad ibn Zurarah. Back in Yathrib they called their people to Islam, until there was not a household of the Ansar, as the people of Yathrib came to be known, in which the Prophet ﷺ was not talked about.[[03:p1–p3, 04:p1–p4 | Ibn Ishaq, "as has been related to me": the six and their names]]

## Twelve men and a teacher

The next year twelve men of the Ansar met him at al-‘Aqabah and made the first pledge, before fighting had been prescribed.[[05:p1 | Ibn Ishaq]] One of them, ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit, remembered its terms: to associate nothing with Allah, not to steal, commit adultery or kill their children, not to spread slander, and not to disobey him in what was right. "If you keep to this, Paradise is yours."[[06:p1–p2 | ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit, through Yazid ibn Abi Habib]]

When they went home, the Prophet ﷺ sent Mus‘ab ibn ‘Umayr with them to teach them the Quran and the religion. In Yathrib they called him "the reciter". He stayed with As‘ad and led the Muslims in prayer, since some of Aws and Khazraj, the town's two tribes, did not want to be led by a man of the other.[[08:p1, 09:p1 | Ibn Ishaq]]

## A spear planted in the ground

One day As‘ad brought Mus‘ab to an orchard among Banu ‘Abd al-Ashhal. The clan's two chiefs, Sa‘d ibn Mu‘adh and Usayd ibn Hudayr, still followed their people's religion, and Sa‘d sent Usayd to drive the visitors away.[[10:p1–p2, 11:p2 | ‘Ubayd Allah ibn al-Mughirah and ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr]]

Usayd stood over them with his spear, cursing. "Will you not sit and listen?" Mus‘ab asked. "If you like it, accept it. If you dislike it, we will keep it away from you." "That is fair," said Usayd. He planted his spear in the ground and sat. As Mus‘ab recited the Quran, Islam showed in Usayd's face before he said a word. "How beautiful these words are!" He washed, bore witness to the truth and prayed. When Sa‘d saw him walking back, he said: "By Allah, Usayd has come back to you with a different face from the one he left with." Usayd sent Sa‘d to them with a story that his cousin As‘ad was in danger.[[10:p1–p2, 11:p2 | ‘Ubayd Allah ibn al-Mughirah and ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr]]

Sa‘d came in anger, spear in hand, and was made the same offer. He too planted the spear, listened, and became Muslim. His clan, seeing him come, said the same of him. He asked them, "What is my standing among you?" "You are our chief." "Then I will not speak to a man or woman of you until you believe in Allah and His Messenger." By evening every man and woman of Banu ‘Abd al-Ashhal was Muslim. Before long there were Muslims in every household of the Ansar but a few clans of Aws.[[12:p1, 13:p1 | ‘Ubayd Allah ibn al-Mughirah and ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr]]

## A night in the pass

At the next pilgrimage the Muslims of Yathrib travelled with their people's idol-worshipping pilgrims.[[14:p1 | Ibn Ishaq]] Two of them, Ka‘b ibn Malik and their elder al-Bara' ibn Ma‘rur, had never seen the Prophet ﷺ. In Makkah they were told to look for the man sitting with his uncle al-‘Abbas in the mosque. When al-‘Abbas named Ka‘b, the Prophet ﷺ said, "The poet?" Ka‘b never forgot it.[[15:p1–p2, 16:p1–p7 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting the Prophet ﷺ]]

They arranged to meet him at al-‘Aqabah on the middle one of the days of Tashriq, the last days of the pilgrimage.[[14:p1 | Ibn Ishaq]][[17:p1–p2, 18:p1 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, through his sons]] When a third of the night had passed, they slipped out of their camps "quietly like sandgrouse," Ka‘b ibn Malik remembered, and gathered in the pass: seventy-three men and two women.[[17:p1–p2, 18:p1 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, through his sons]]

The Prophet ﷺ came with his uncle al-‘Abbas, who still followed his people's religion but wanted to make his nephew's position secure. "He has honour among his people and safety in his town," al-‘Abbas told them. "If you will protect him, the burden is yours. If you will hand him over once he is with you, leave him now."[[19:p1, 20:p1 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Abd al-Muttalib]]

The Prophet ﷺ recited the Quran, then said: "I take your pledge that you will protect me as you protect your women and children." Al-Bara' ibn Ma‘rur took his hand. "Yes, by Him who sent you with the truth. We are sons of war." Abu al-Haytham ibn al-Tayyihan asked: once Allah gave him victory, would he go back to his own people and leave them? The Prophet ﷺ smiled. "I am of you and you are of me. I fight whom you fight, and make peace with whom you make peace."[[21:p1–p4 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting the Prophet ﷺ, al-Bara' and Abu al-Haytham]] He asked for twelve leaders to answer for their people, and they chose nine from Khazraj and three from Aws.[[22:p2–p4 | Ka‘b ibn Malik]]

One of the Ansar, al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah, made the cost plain: they were pledging to fight people of every kind for him, and if they would give him up when their wealth was ruined and their nobles killed, they should leave him now. "We take him, whatever it costs," they said. "What is ours if we keep faith, Messenger of Allah?" "Paradise." "Stretch out your hand." He stretched it out, and they pledged.[[24:p1–p4 | ‘Asim ibn ‘Umar ibn Qatadah, quoting al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah and the Prophet ﷺ]]

## Brothers and a home

"Go back to your camps," the Prophet ﷺ told them. Al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah offered to fall on the people of Mina with their swords the next day. "We have not been commanded to do that," he answered.[[26:p1–p3, 27:p1–p3 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting the Prophet ﷺ]]

In the morning the leading men of Quraysh came asking about the pledge. The idol-worshippers among the pilgrims swore that nothing had happened, and they spoke the truth, for they knew nothing of it. As the Qurayshi men rose, Ka‘b, as if joining in the talk, admired aloud the new sandals of the young al-Harith ibn Hisham. Al-Harith pulled them off and threw them to him, and Ka‘b would not give them back: "It is a good omen."[[28:p1 | Ka‘b ibn Malik]] But once the pilgrims had left, Quraysh learned it was true and went after them. They caught Sa‘d ibn ‘Ubadah, one of the twelve leaders, tied his hands to his neck and brought him into Makkah, beating him and dragging him by his long hair.[[31:p1 | ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr, continued]] He was saved by an old bond: in Yathrib he used to protect the merchants of two men of Quraysh, Jubayr ibn Mut‘im and al-Harith ibn Harb. He called out their names, and they came and freed him.[[H-23833-472-prep-747, H-23833-473-prep-cont | Sa‘d ibn ‘Ubadah's own words]]

A people had now pledged to stand by the Prophet ﷺ and all who followed him. In Makkah, the believers could begin to go.

## In depth

### The Pledge of the Women

Ibn Ishaq calls the first pledge the "Pledge of the Women" and says it came before fighting was prescribed.[[05:p1 | Ibn Ishaq]] The edition's editor explains the name from the Quran, where women pledge "that they will associate nothing with Allah" (al-Mumtahanah 60:12): the twelve pledged faith and conduct, not war.[[H-23833-454-prep-foot-2]] ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit gives its terms in two versions. In one, anyone who breaks them is left to Allah, who may punish or forgive; in the other, a prescribed punishment suffered in this world is an expiation, and only what stays hidden is left to Allah.[[06:p1–p2 | ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit, through Yazid ibn Abi Habib]][[07:p1–p2 | ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit, through al-Zuhri and Abu Idris al-Khawlani]] At the second meeting ‘Ubadah, by then one of the twelve leaders, speaks instead of "the pledge of war": to hear and obey in hardship and ease, not to dispute authority with those who hold it, and to speak the truth wherever they were, fearing no one's blame for Allah's sake.[[H-23833-477-AQ-NAMES-CONTEXT | ‘Ubadah ibn al-Samit, through his grandson ‘Ubadah ibn al-Walid]]

### "We have not been commanded to do that"

Ibn Ishaq explains that before the pledge at al-‘Aqabah the Prophet ﷺ had not been given permission to fight; he had been told to call people to Allah, to bear harm patiently and to forgive the ignorant. Permission came after Quraysh had tormented and driven out his followers, with the verse "Permission has been given to those who are fought, because they were wronged" (al-Hajj 22:39), as he heard from ‘Urwah ibn al-Zubayr and other scholars.[[H-23833-490-prep-777 | Ibn Ishaq, from ‘Urwah ibn al-Zubayr and other scholars]] His order to emigrate follows that permission and the Ansar's pledge.[[H-23833-491-prep-778 | Ibn Ishaq]]

### Why did al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah speak?

The warning about losing wealth and nobles comes from ‘Asim ibn ‘Umar ibn Qatadah, who said al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah meant to bind the pledge tighter on their necks. ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr said he wanted to delay them that night in the hope that ‘Abdullah ibn Ubayy would come and strengthen their cause. Ibn Ishaq leaves it open: "Allah knows best which it was." This al-‘Abbas, one of the Ansar, is a different man from the Prophet's ﷺ uncle al-‘Abbas, who spoke at the start of the meeting.[[24:p5–p7 | ‘Asim ibn ‘Umar ibn Qatadah and ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr]][[19:p1, 20:p1 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Abd al-Muttalib]]

### The twelve leaders

‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr adds the Prophet's ﷺ charge to the twelve: "You are guarantors for your people and what concerns them, as the disciples were guarantors for Jesus son of Mary, and I am guarantor for my people." They answered, "Yes."[[23:p1–p4 | ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr, quoting the Prophet ﷺ]] Two of them, Sa‘d ibn ‘Ubadah and al-Mundhir ibn ‘Amr, were the men Quraysh caught on the road; al-Mundhir got away.[[31:p1 | ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr, continued]]

### The morning after

When Quraysh put the same question to ‘Abdullah ibn Ubayy, he said: "This is a grave matter. My people would not have done anything like this without me. I knew nothing of it."[[28:p2–p3, 29:p1 | ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr]]

### Who struck his hand first?

Each clan remembered its own man. Banu al-Najjar said As‘ad ibn Zurarah was the first to strike the Prophet's ﷺ hand in pledge; Banu ‘Abd al-Ashhal said Abu al-Haytham ibn al-Tayyihan; Ka‘b ibn Malik said al-Bara' ibn Ma‘rur.[[30:p1–p3 | Ibn Ishaq]]

### Al-Bara' and the prayer direction

On the road to that pilgrimage, al-Bara' ibn Ma‘rur, the elder of the group, decided to pray towards the Ka‘bah while his companions prayed towards Syria, as the Prophet ﷺ then did. In Makkah, when they found the Prophet ﷺ sitting with al-‘Abbas in the mosque, al-Bara' asked him about it. "You had a prayer direction, if only you had kept to it," he answered, and al-Bara' went back to praying towards Syria with them. Ka‘b rejects his family's claim that he prayed towards the Ka‘bah until he died.[[15:p1–p2, 16:p1–p7 | Ka‘b ibn Malik, quoting the Prophet ﷺ]]

### "Blood for blood"

The Prophet's ﷺ answer to Abu al-Haytham also includes the words "al-hadm for al-hadm". Ibn Hisham reports that some explained al-hadm as inviolability: "my protection is your protection".[[21:p4, 22:p1 | the Prophet ﷺ in Ka‘b's account]]

### The voice from the top of al-‘Aqabah

Ka‘b says it was Satan who screamed from the top of al-‘Aqabah, calling the Prophet ﷺ "Mudhammam", "the reviled one", and his followers "renegades". The Prophet ﷺ named him and said: "Do you hear, enemy of Allah? By Allah, I will deal with you."[[25:p1–p6 | Ka‘b ibn Malik]]

### Before the six

An earlier meeting with men of Yathrib is told by Mahmud ibn Labid. Young men of Banu ‘Abd al-Ashhal, a clan of Aws, came to Makkah seeking an alliance with Quraysh against Khazraj, and the Prophet ﷺ offered them "something better than what you came for". Iyas ibn Mu‘adh, a youth among them, said: "This, by Allah, is better than what we came for." Their leader threw a handful of dust in his face, and they went home. The battle of Bu‘ath between Aws and Khazraj followed. Iyas died soon after, and those with him heard him praising Allah until the end.[[H-23833-450-prep-711, H-23833-451-prep-cont | Ibn Ishaq from al-Husayn ibn ‘Abd al-Rahman from Mahmud ibn Labid]]

### How long did Madinah wait?

Al-Zuhri said the pledge on the night of al-‘Aqabah was in Dhu al-Hijjah, and that about three months passed between it and the Prophet's ﷺ emigration; he reached Madinah in Rabi‘ al-Awwal.[[H-7478-1032-RA-DATE-ZUHRI | al-Zuhri, through ‘Uqayl and al-Layth, from Ahmad ibn Hanbal]]

## For the reviewer

- **Decisions to confirm.** (1) The account calls the town Yathrib until the last paragraph, where the Prophet ﷺ sends his companions to "Madinah"; the passages themselves call it al-Madinah throughout (STYLE_GUIDE: Yathrib only before the Hijrah). (2) "The Ansar, as the Muslims of Yathrib came to be called" glosses a term the passages use from the start; it adds no event. (3) The voice from the top of al-‘Aqabah, which Ka‘b says was Satan's, is told only in In depth; the account goes straight from the pledge to the order to return to the camps. (4) The chapter is a step back in time from `hch_early_migration`: it opens with one bridging sentence in the overview (the emigrants went to a town that had made room for them) and ends where that chapter begins, with the order to emigrate and the Prophet ﷺ waiting for permission, which hands over to `hch_preparations`. The emigrants' journeys themselves are not retold. (5) Mus‘ab's arrangement of the two conversions is told as the two transmitters tell it, including Usayd's ruse about Banu Harithah, condensed to "he must hurry to his cousin's aid".
- **New readings:** Sa‘d ibn ‘Ubadah freed (note 21); the order to emigrate (22); the editor's note on the Pledge of the Women (24); ‘Ubadah's "pledge of war" (25); permission to fight (30); al-Zuhri's three months (31); Iyas ibn Mu‘adh and Bu‘ath (32). All are in `evidence.jsonl`.
- **Length.** The account is about 1,200 words after the details pass, over the ~900 target: the episode spans three pilgrimage seasons, two pledges, Mus‘ab's year in Yathrib and Quraysh's pursuit, and each is needed to reach the hand-over. The likeliest further cut is the Usayd and Sa‘d section.
- **Gaps:** none that the story needs. The account does not say where al-‘Aqabah lies beyond Mina's camps, because the passages give nothing more.
- **Removed from the account:** genealogies and Ibn Hisham's notes on names; ‘Ubadah's two versions, al-Bara' and the prayer direction, the first to strike the hand, and the two readings of al-‘Abbas ibn ‘Ubadah's purpose (all in In depth); Abu Qays's name and poem; the names of Abu Jabir and the two women at the pledge; al-Bara''s and al-‘Abbas's longer speeches (condensed); the guarantors' charge and ‘Abdullah ibn Ubayy's denial (moved to In depth). The details pass brought into the account: the Jews' "‘Ad and Iram", the "different face" of Usayd and Sa‘d, Ka‘b's first meeting ("The poet?"), the date of the meeting, and the sandals.
