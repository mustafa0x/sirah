# A New Home in Madinah

chapter: hch_settlement
stage: arrival

**Question:** How did Madinah become home for the Prophet ﷺ, his family and the emigrants from Makkah?

## Overview

The Prophet ﷺ had found a place in Madinah, and his mosque was going up.[^5] Yet his wife and daughters were still in Makkah, and so was Abu Bakr's family.[^2][^3] One by one, the last of the emigrants were making their way to join him.[^1] Madinah had welcomed them; making it home would be harder. The town was known for its fever, and it soon laid the newcomers low, leaving them longing for the valleys and springs of Makkah.[^31][^18][^20]

## Families on the road

The emigrants kept coming. They followed one another to the Prophet ﷺ until none of them was left in Makkah except those held there against their will or forced from their faith. A few clans took their whole households and wealth, and their houses in Makkah stood shut, with no one living in them.[^1]

From Abu Ayyub's house, the Prophet ﷺ sent Zayd ibn Harithah and Abu Rafi‘ to Makkah with two camels and five hundred dirhams, to bring his family.[^2] ‘Abdullah ibn Urayqit was returning to Makkah, and Abu Bakr sent him to his son ‘Abdullah, telling him to come with the household.[^3][^4]

They came together: Sawdah bint Zam‘ah, the Prophet's ﷺ wife; his daughters Fatimah and Umm Kulthum; Umm Ayman, Zayd's wife, with her son Usamah; and ‘Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr with his mother Umm Ruman and his sisters ‘Aishah and Asma.[^5][^2] Two daughters were not among them. Ruqayyah had gone before with her husband, ‘Uthman ibn ‘Affan, and Zaynab, the eldest, stayed behind in Makkah, held back by her husband, Abu al-‘As ibn al-Rabi‘.[^6][^2]

They arrived while the Prophet ﷺ was still building his mosque,[^5] and he lodged them in the house of Harithah ibn al-Nu‘man.[^2]

## A child born at Quba

Asma was expecting a child. "I set out when my pregnancy was at full term," she said. "I came to Madinah, stopped at Quba, and gave birth to him at Quba."[^7]

Then she brought the baby, ‘Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, to the Prophet ﷺ and laid him in his lap. He called for a date, chewed it and put it into the baby's mouth, so that the first thing to enter the child's stomach was the Prophet's ﷺ own saliva. He rubbed the date on the baby's palate, prayed for him and asked Allah to bless him.[^7][^8]

"He was the first child born in Islam," Asma said,[^7] meaning, Ibn Hajar explains, the first born to the emigrants in Madinah.[^9]

## Brothers, and the first graves

The Prophet ﷺ made the emigrants brothers to one another, and brothers to the Ansar, the Muslims of Madinah who had taken them in. Each pair was bound to stand by the other in what was right and to share what they had, and at first such brothers even inherited from one another, ahead of their own kin. Ibn Sa‘d puts them at ninety men, forty-five from each side; others said a hundred.[^11] It was done, Anas ibn Malik said, in his house.[^12]

Those months also brought loss. Al-Bara ibn Ma‘rur had died in Safar, a month before the Prophet ﷺ came, asking to be laid in his grave facing the Ka‘bah. When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Madinah, he prayed at his grave.[^13] His host Kulthum ibn al-Hidm lived only a short while after his coming, al-Tabari reports.[^14] Then As‘ad ibn Zurarah died while the mosque was still being built, taken by a sickness of the throat. He was the first Muslim to be buried in al-Baqi‘, the burial ground of Madinah.[^15][^14]

When the mosque was finished, the Prophet ﷺ left Abu Ayyub's house and moved into the dwelling built for him there.[^15]

## The fever of Madinah

"We came to Madinah when it was a land of plague," ‘Aishah said;[^16] in another telling, "the most plague-ridden land of Allah."[^17] Abu Bakr fell ill, and so did Bilal. ‘Aishah went in to see them. "Father, how do you feel? Bilal, how do you feel?" Whenever the fever took hold of Abu Bakr, he would say:

> Every man is wished good morning among his family,
> and death is nearer to him than the strap of his sandal.[^18]

"By Allah, my father does not know what he is saying," ‘Aishah said. In Ibn Ishaq's telling she then went to ‘Amir ibn Fuhayrah and asked how he was, and he too answered in verse: "I have found death before I tasted it."[^19]

When the fever left Bilal, he would raise his voice:

> If only I knew whether I will spend a night again
> in a valley, with idhkhir and jalil grass around me,
> and whether one day I will come down to the waters of Majannah,
> and whether Shamah and Tafil will rise before me.[^18]

All of them were places near Makkah.[^20] Then he would call down a curse on the men he blamed: "O Allah, curse ‘Utbah ibn Rabi‘ah, Shaybah ibn Rabi‘ah and Umayyah ibn Khalaf, as they drove us out to a land of plague."[^21]

‘Aishah fell ill herself. The fever kept her down for a month, and her hair fell out.[^22][^23]

## A prayer for the city

‘Aishah went to the Prophet ﷺ and told him what she had seen.[^18] "Messenger of Allah," she said, "they are raving and do not know what they are saying, so severe is their fever."[^19]

In Makkah he had once said that it was the dearest of Allah's lands to him, and that he would never have left it had he not been driven out.[^25] Now he prayed for the town that had taken them in:

"O Allah, make Madinah dear to us as You made Makkah dear to us, or dearer. Make it healthy. Bless us in its sa‘ and its mudd, the measures of our food. And take its fever away to al-Juhfah."[^16][^18]

## In depth

### Who came, and when?

The lists differ in small ways. The clans that left Makkah with their whole households, Ibn Ishaq says, were Banu Maz‘un, Banu Jahsh and Banu al-Bukayr.[^1] Al-Tabari adds that Talhah ibn ‘Ubaydullah travelled with Abu Bakr's family.[^4] Ibn Ishaq, as Ibn Hajar cites him, names Asma among Abu Bakr's household; Ibn Hibban names ‘Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr in the household instead; Ibn Sa‘d has the two messengers carry two camels and five hundred dirhams, and lodges the families in the house of Harithah ibn al-Nu‘man.[^3][^5][^2] Ibn Ishaq has them arrive while the mosque was being built;[^5] Ibn Hibban tells of the mission after the Prophet ﷺ had moved into his dwelling.[^15][^3] Al-Tabari introduces Ibn Urayqit's return and Talhah's company with "as is reported".[^4] ‘Abdullah ibn Urayqit is the name Ibn Hisham gives for the guide who led the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr to Madinah.

### When was ‘Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr born?

Asma gives a place, Quba, but no date. Ibn Hajar takes her report, together with the families' arrival during the building of the mosque, to show that the child was born in the first year after the Hijrah. He sets aside al-Waqidi's dating to the second year, twenty months after the Hijrah: the household of Abu Bakr came soon after the Prophet ﷺ had settled, he says, too soon for a delay of twenty months, or even ten.[^26][^27][^5] Her stopping at Quba, he adds, does not mean she reached Madinah before the Prophet ﷺ had left Quba.[^9] The "first child born in Islam" is the first born to the emigrants in Madinah; others are named for Abyssinia and for the Ansar.[^9] Al-Bukhari also gives a short version from ‘Aishah, and a supporting route saying that Asma migrated while pregnant.[^8][^28] One telling of Asma's report, al-Isma‘ili's, adds that the Muslims rejoiced greatly at the birth, because the Jews had been saying, "We have put a spell on them, so that no child will be born to them."[^10]

### The brotherhood

Ibn Sa‘d's report says the pact of brotherhood came before the battle of Badr, and that inheritance between brothers ended when the verse "And blood relatives are nearer to one another in the Book of Allah" (al-Anfal 8:75) was revealed.[^11] Ibn Hibban gives a longer narrative, without a chain, placing the brotherhood between emigrants and Ansar in Ramadan and pairing companions by name: Abu Bakr with ‘Umar, ‘Uthman with ‘Abd al-Rahman ibn ‘Awf, Talhah with al-Zubayr, Sa‘d ibn Abi Waqqas with ‘Ammar ibn Yasir, Abu al-Darda with Salman.[^29]

### The verses of the fever

Abu Bakr's line, Bilal's verses and the prayer come from ‘Aishah in al-Bukhari, through Malik.[^18] ‘Amir ibn Fuhayrah's verses and ‘Aishah's words "they are raving" are an addition Ibn Ishaq transmits, which Ibn Hajar quotes; Malik has ‘Amir's verse in *al-Muwatta* with a broken chain.[^19] Bilal's curse is in another route of the same hadith that al-Bukhari gives at the end of the Book of Pilgrimage; Ibn Hibban's version names Abu Sufyan and Abu Jahl instead of Umayyah.[^21][^30] Ibn Hibban adds a scene without a chain: the Prophet ﷺ came into the mosque and found people praying sitting down, struck by the fever. "The prayer of one who sits is half the prayer of one who stands," he told them, and they finished it on their feet. Then he said: "O Allah, place in Madinah twice the blessing that is in Makkah."[^24] Ibn Hajar places Majannah a few miles from Makkah, where there was a market, and Shamah and Tafil as two mountains near Makkah; al-Khattabi came to think they were two springs.[^20]

### Madinah's fever and al-Juhfah

Hisham ibn ‘Urwah, through Ibn Ishaq, says the town's fever was known before Islam: a newcomer who wanted to escape it was told to bray like a donkey.[^31] The note in the edition of Muslim explains "a land of plague" as an unhealthy land where illness is common, especially for strangers; it adds al-Khattabi's remark that al-Juhfah was then inhabited by Jews, and al-Nawawi's that al-Juhfah was avoided from that day on.[^32] Al-Bukhari also records the prayer in a shorter wording, and Muslim in a form close to Malik's.[^33][^16]

## For the reviewer

- **Decisions to confirm.** (1) Title changed to "A New Home in Madinah", to land the book; the old title's "families follow, and a prayer for the city" is now the account's arc. (2) The book ends on the prayer for Madinah, set against the Prophet's ﷺ words to Makkah at al-Hazwara from the departure chapter (note 25); the callback does not date those words. (3) The brotherhood is told in one short paragraph from Ibn Sa‘d and Anas (new readings); Ibn Hibban's long chainless narrative, with its named pairs and sayings, is kept only in In depth. Drop the paragraph if the brotherhood should belong to no chapter or another one. (4) The move from Abu Ayyub's house into the new dwelling is one sentence; the arrival and mosque chapters also touch it, so the sequence plan should give it to one of them. (5) Al-Isma‘ili's addition about the Jews' claim of a spell is kept with attribution as the source words it, because it explains the Muslims' joy; cut it if it is judged too much for the reading flow. (6) ‘Amir ibn Fuhayrah's verse and ‘Aishah's "they are raving" come from Ibn Ishaq's addition (Malik's route is broken) and are attributed once. (7) Ibn Hibban's mosque scene (praying sitting; "twice the blessing") is chainless, so it sits in In depth rather than the account. (8) Abu Bakr's household is named as in Ibn Ishaq (Umm Ruman, ‘Aishah, Asma); ‘Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr, named only by Ibn Hibban, is in In depth.
- **New readings:** al-Tabari on Ibn Urayqit's return and Talhah (4) and on the first deaths (14); the start of As‘ad ibn Zurarah's name in Ibn Hibban (15); Ibn Sa‘d and Anas on the brotherhood (11–12); ‘Aishah's own fever in Muslim and al-Bukhari (22–23); Ibn Hibban's mosque scene (24) and fever version (30); the al-Hazwara words from the departure chapter (25).
- **Gaps:** no reading dates the fever or the prayer more closely than "when the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came to Madinah"; none gives the families' route or how long they travelled. The In depth line naming Ibn Urayqit as the guide rests on Ibn Hisham's remark quoted in the road chapter, not on a reading in this chapter.
- **Removed from the account:** chains of narrators; the commentators' notes on spelling and on al-Juhfah; the dispute over the birth date; the Jahiliyyah custom of braying (now in In depth); Ibn Hibban's named brotherhood pairs and his sayings about individual companions; the Muslim edition's note that sets al-Nawawi's and al-Khattabi's remarks apart from the prayer.
