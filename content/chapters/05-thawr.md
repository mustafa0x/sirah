# Three Nights at Thawr

chapter: hch_thawr
stage: thawr

**Question:** How did the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr stay hidden in the cave at Thawr while Quraysh hunted for them?

## Overview

The Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr had slipped out of Makkah by night, heading not north to Madinah but south, to a mountain called Thawr.[^2] There, in a cave, they would wait three nights.[^3] By morning Quraysh would know he was gone, and they would send riders in every direction, with a great reward for whoever brought him back.[^7][^8] Everything now depended on a handful of people who knew where they were, and on what the searchers would see when they reached the mountain.[^6][^14]

## The way to the cave

Abu Bakr could not walk calmly beside him. In a report Muhammad ibn Sirin gives from ‘Umar, he kept moving, at times ahead of the Prophet ﷺ and at times behind him, until the Prophet ﷺ asked why. "Messenger of Allah," he said, "I think of those coming after you, and walk behind you; then I think of those lying in wait, and walk in front of you."[^1]

About five miles south of Makkah they reached Mount Thawr,[^2] and a cave in it,[^3] an opening in the mountainside.[^4] They came to it at night.[^5] "Stay where you are, Messenger of Allah," Abu Bakr said, "until I have checked the cave for you."[^1] He went in first and felt his way around it, to see whether a wild animal or a snake was inside, ready to take the harm himself.[^5] Then the Prophet ﷺ came in after him. For three nights, this would be their hiding place.[^3]

## Makkah wakes

The men who had watched the Prophet's ﷺ door all night rushed in at dawn and found ‘Ali in his bed. "Where is this companion of yours?" they asked. "I do not know," he said.[^6] Quraysh offered a hundred camels to whoever brought him back to them.[^7] They rode out in every direction looking for him, and sent word to the people at the watering places, promising them a great reward.[^8]

Some of them came to Abu Bakr's house. Asma, his daughter, went out to them, and they asked where her father was. "By Allah, I do not know where my father is," she said. Abu Jahl, a coarse and brutal man, raised his hand and struck her across the face so hard that her earring flew off. Ibn Ishaq heard this from someone who reported it from Asma.[^9]

## Feet at the mouth of the cave

Others followed the Prophet's ﷺ tracks out of the city. At the mountain the trail became confused, so they climbed it, and passed by the cave.[^6] They came right above it, close enough for the two inside to hear their voices.[^8] Abu Bakr looked up and saw their feet over his head. "Messenger of Allah," he said, "if one of them looked at his feet, he would see us beneath his feet." The Prophet ﷺ answered: "Abu Bakr, what do you think of two when Allah is their third?"[^10][^11]

Abu Bakr was overcome with worry and fear. The Prophet ﷺ told him, "Do not grieve; Allah is with us," and he prayed, and calm from Allah came down on him.[^8] In a report from Ibn ‘Abbas, the searchers saw a spider's web across the entrance and said, "If anyone had gone in here, there would be no spider's web over its entrance."[^6] Allah blinded their eyes to him, and at last the searchers gave up and went back.[^12]

The Quran recalls that moment: "If you do not help him, Allah has already helped him, when those who disbelieved drove him out, the second of two, when the two were in the cave, when he said to his companion, 'Do not grieve; Allah is with us.' Then Allah sent down His tranquility upon him, and supported him with forces you did not see."[^13]

## Visitors after dark

The two were not cut off. Abu Bakr had given his son and his freedman their tasks.[^15] His son ‘Abdullah, a sharp young man who was quick to learn, spent each night with them in the cave and left in the last hours before dawn. By morning he was among Quraysh in Makkah, as if he had slept there. All day he listened to what they said and planned against the two, and kept every word of it in his head; when darkness closed in, he was back at the cave with the news.[^14][^16]

Abu Bakr's freedman, ‘Amir ibn Fuhayrah, grazed a flock of milking sheep among the shepherds of Makkah, where no one paid him any attention.[^16][^17] Some time after nightfall he brought the flock to the cave, and the two slept with fresh milk; in the dark before dawn he called the sheep and drove them off again.[^14] When ‘Abdullah set out for Makkah in the morning, ‘Amir drove the flock along his trail, so that the sheep wiped out his footprints.[^16] Every evening, too, Asma brought them food.[^15] So it went, every one of the three nights.[^14]

## The third night

At last the three nights passed, and the talk about the two died down.[^16][^17] On the morning after the third night, the guide they had hired, ‘Abdullah ibn Urayqit, came to the cave as arranged, with their two camels and a third of his own.[^16][^18][^20]

Asma came too, carrying their bag of food.[^16] The camels were ready, and the road to Madinah lay ahead.[^18]

## In depth

### "What do you think of two…?"

Abu Bakr's words in the cave come from Abu Bakr himself, through Anas ibn Malik, and both al-Bukhari and Muslim record them. The tellings differ slightly. In Muslim, Abu Bakr saw "the feet of the idolaters above our heads" and feared one of them would look "at his feet"; the Prophet ﷺ answered, "Abu Bakr, what do you think of two when Allah is their third?"[^11] In al-Bukhari, Abu Bakr raised his head, saw the men's feet and feared one of them would "lower his gaze"; the reply begins "Be quiet, Abu Bakr: two, and Allah is their third."[^10] Another chain in al-Bayhaqi has "if one of them raised his foot."[^21] The story above follows Muslim's wording. The calm that came down, and the words "Do not grieve", are from ‘Urwah ibn al-Zubayr's account, which uses the words of the verse.[^8]

### On whom did the tranquility come down?

The verse says Allah "sent down His tranquility upon him." Ibn ‘Abbas held that this meant Abu Bakr, because the Prophet ﷺ had never been without it; others said it meant the Prophet ﷺ.[^22] For the "forces you did not see", al-Baghawi gives angels turning the searchers' faces and eyes away from him, or fear cast into their hearts until they went back.[^23]

### The spider's web

The web over the entrance comes from a report of Ibn ‘Abbas recorded by Ahmad. Ibn Kathir called its chain hasan (good) and "among the best of what is reported about the spider's web over the mouth of the cave", and Ibn Hajar also called it hasan.[^24] The modern hadith scholar al-Albani disagreed: one narrator, ‘Uthman al-Jazari, is weak in his judgment. He also argued that the verse speaks of forces "you did not see", which a spider's web is not.[^25] That is why the story gives the web as Ibn ‘Abbas's report rather than as settled fact.

Other stories of the cave did not hold up. A longer report adds a tree that grew across the entrance and two wild pigeons nesting there; al-Albani judged it munkar (rejected), and Muhammad al-‘Awshan notes that no scholar he knew of authenticated the pigeons.[^26] A story of Abu Bakr blocking a hole with his foot and being stung by snakes was judged by al-Dhahabi to resemble the inventions of street storytellers.[^27]

### How the way to the cave is known

Abu Bakr walking ahead of and behind the Prophet ﷺ, and asking him to wait while he checked the cave, come from Muhammad ibn Sirin, who reports what ‘Umar used to say: that one night of Abu Bakr's was better than the whole family of ‘Umar.[^1] Abu Bakr feeling his way around the cave for an animal or a snake comes from al-Hasan al-Basri, a report that reached Ibn Hisham from "some scholars" he does not name.[^5] Asma's encounter with Abu Jahl is also a single report: Ibn Ishaq says only that he "was told" it from Asma.[^9]

### Three nights, or two?

‘Aishah's account in al-Bukhari, Ibn Ishaq, Mujahid and al-Zuhri all give three nights.[^3][^7][^28] In one version of ‘Urwah's account, recorded by al-Bayhaqi, the two stayed two days and two nights, while Musa ibn ‘Uqbah's version has three.[^17] Later biographers put dates to the stay. Al-Mubarakfuri, a modern biographer, names the nights of Friday, Saturday and Sunday, with the guide arriving on the night of Monday, the first of Rabi‘ al-Awwal.[^29] Al-Samhudi cites Ibn al-Kalbi for the same night of leaving the cave.[^30]

### Asma's waistband

Two places are given for Asma's waistband. In ‘Aishah's account and Asma's own, she cut or split it at Abu Bakr's house while the provisions were being prepared (see the chapter on the preparations).[^31] In Ibn Ishaq's account, she untied it at the cave, when the two were about to leave. Ibn Ishaq gives her name as "the woman of the waistband"; Ibn Hisham heard scholars call her "the woman of the two waistbands", because she split it in two, hanging the bag with one half and wearing the other.[^19]

### The guide's name

‘Aishah's account in al-Bukhari calls him only a man of Banu al-Dil, a skilled guide.[^18] Ibn Ishaq names him ‘Abdullah ibn Arqat; Ibn Hisham adds that he is also called ‘Abdullah ibn Urayqit, the name Ibn Sa‘d gives.[^20]

## For the reviewer

- **Decisions to confirm.** (1) The spider's web is told in the account, attributed once to Ibn ‘Abbas, with the grading dispute (Ibn Kathir and Ibn Hajar: hasan; al-Albani: weak) in In depth. The tree, the pigeons and the snake bite are left out of the account and named in In depth as rejected. (2) Abu Bakr's words in the cave follow Muslim's wording (it matches the approved caption N03d); al-Bukhari's "Be quiet, Abu Bakr" is in In depth, not merged into one quotation. (3) Three single or later reports are told with a short attribution: Ibn Sirin from ‘Umar (the walk and checking the cave), al-Hasan al-Basri (feeling for animals or snakes), and Asma and Abu Jahl ("Ibn Ishaq heard this from someone…"). (4) Ibn Ishaq's hundred camels and ‘Urwah's "great reward" are told here as part of the search. The Suraqah chapter opens on Quraysh's reward in Suraqah's own wording, so it should refer back to this chapter in a sentence rather than retell it. (5) The guide is named ‘Abdullah ibn Urayqit (Ibn Hisham's "also called", and Ibn Sa‘d); al-Bukhari does not name him. (6) Asma's waistband is told at the cave, following Ibn Ishaq. The preparations chapter tells the al-Bukhari version (at Abu Bakr's house), so In depth explains that the reports place it differently. (7) The search is told as the first morning, following Ibn ‘Abbas ("they followed his tracks" after finding ‘Ali), and the nightly visits come afterwards as the pattern of all three nights.
- **New readings:** Ibn Ishaq on the family's tasks, the reward, the covered tracks, the guide's third camel and Asma's waistband (notes 7, 15, 16, 19); ‘Urwah in al-Bayhaqi (8, 17); Ibn Sirin from ‘Umar (1); al-Hasan al-Basri (5); Asma and Abu Jahl (9); Ibn ‘Abbas on the tracks and the web, with its gradings (6, 24–25); Ibn Hibban on the searchers turning back (12); al-Baghawi on the cave and the tranquility (4, 22–23); the guide's name (20); the rejected stories and their critics (26–27); three nights in al-Tabari (28); dates (29–30); the road south (2).
- **Gaps.** (1) No reading in the archive explains *radif* (in ‘Aishah's "milk… and their radif"), so the story says only "fresh milk".
